Grappling vs BJJ — What’s the Difference?

Same roots, different game — here's how to tell them apart

If you've been researching martial arts, you've probably come across both "grappling" and "BJJ" and wondered what separates them. The short answer: they share the same DNA, but the equipment, rules and culture differ in ways that matter. Here's the full picture.

What they have in common

Both no-gi grappling and Brazilian jiu-jitsu (BJJ) are ground-based combat sports. Both focus on controlling an opponent and forcing a submission using joint locks and chokes. Neither involves striking. At a technical level, many of the positions, sweeps, escapes and submissions are identical — a rear naked choke works the same way whether you're wearing a gi or not.

BJJ was itself developed from judo in Brazil in the early 20th century, with a heavy emphasis on ground fighting. No-gi grappling draws heavily from BJJ, wrestling and other combat sports, with the gi removed.

The key difference: the gi

The gi is a heavy cotton jacket and trousers worn in traditional BJJ (and judo). It's not just clothing — it's part of the game. Grapplers use grips on the collar, sleeves and lapels as control and attack tools. Many BJJ techniques are built entirely around gi grips.

In no-gi grappling, there is no jacket. Practitioners wear shorts and a rashguard (a close-fitting athletic top). Without fabric to grip, the game changes in several important ways:

  • Faster pace — grips on skin and clothing are harder to maintain than grips on a gi, so the game tends to be more dynamic and scramble-heavy
  • Different control positions — no-gi places greater emphasis on body locks, underhooks and head control rather than fabric grips
  • Leg lock focus — heel hooks and other leg submissions are a much bigger part of no-gi grappling than traditional gi BJJ, particularly at competition level
  • Wrestling influence — no-gi grappling incorporates more wrestling-style takedowns and scrambles

Rules and competition

Both sports have their own competition formats. BJJ competitions typically award points for positional control and sweeps, with a submission ending the match immediately. No-gi grappling competitions follow similar principles but with ruleset variations — submission-only formats (where only a tap counts) are especially popular in no-gi.

Major no-gi grappling organisations include ADCC (the sport's premier international event), IBJJF No-Gi Worlds, and numerous UK-based promotions. Competition is optional — the majority of people who train never compete, and training is valuable regardless.

Which should I start with?

If there's a gym near you that offers both, try both and see which environment you prefer. If you're choosing between them, here are a few pointers:

  • Choose no-gi if you prefer faster, more athletic training, don't want to invest in a gi straight away, or are primarily interested in self-defence or MMA application
  • Choose gi BJJ if you're drawn to the traditional martial arts structure, want to use grips as a major part of your game, or prefer a slightly more methodical pace
  • Train both if you can — they complement each other well, and most high-level grapplers do both
