What is No-Gi Grappling?

A plain-English introduction to one of the fastest-growing combat sports in the world

No-gi grappling is a combat sport focused entirely on controlling your opponent and forcing a submission — without striking, and without a gi (the traditional jacket worn in judo or BJJ). You wear shorts and a rashguard instead. The goal is to take your opponent down, control them on the ground, and apply a joint lock or choke that makes them tap out.

If you've ever watched the UFC and wondered what's happening when two fighters go to the ground — that's grappling. The sport exists in its own right, with its own competitions, rulebooks and community, and it's growing fast.

What techniques are involved?

No-gi grappling draws from several martial arts disciplines — primarily wrestling, Brazilian jiu-jitsu (BJJ), judo and sambo. In practice, training covers three main areas:

  • Takedowns and trips — getting your opponent from standing to the ground using wrestling and judo-style techniques
  • Positional control — once on the ground, learning to hold dominant positions (back control, mount, side control) and escape from bad ones
  • Submissions — joint locks targeting the arms, legs and shoulders, and chokes targeting the neck. The opponent taps to signal they've had enough

There is no striking of any kind. No-gi grappling is a technical, problem-solving sport — closer to chess than boxing.

What's the difference between no-gi grappling and BJJ?

BJJ (Brazilian jiu-jitsu) is traditionally practised in a gi — a heavy cotton jacket and trousers. Grapplers use grips on the jacket as part of the game. No-gi removes the jacket entirely, which changes the pace and tactics significantly: grips are taken on the body rather than the clothing, making the sport faster and more dynamic.

Many people train both. Some prefer no-gi only. If you're not sure which to start with, our guide to grappling vs BJJ goes into more detail.

Why do people love it?

The sport attracts people from all walks of life for different reasons. Some come for fitness — grappling is a genuinely demanding full-body workout that improves strength, cardio and flexibility simultaneously. Others come for the mental challenge: every roll is a live problem to solve, which makes training addictive in a way that gym machines rarely are.

Many people also find grappling to be one of the most ego-free martial arts environments. Because everyone gets tapped eventually — regardless of size or strength — the culture tends to be humble, supportive and focused on long-term improvement rather than short-term wins.

Is no-gi grappling safe?

Yes — when trained in a well-coached environment. Beginners learn to fall safely, to tap early when caught in a submission, and to work with partners at a controlled pace. No-gi grappling has no striking, which removes one of the main injury risks in combat sports.

The key is finding a gym that prioritises safety culture and takes time with new members. At Forged Grappling, coaches stay on the mat throughout every session and beginners are eased in gradually — you won't be thrown in at the deep end.
